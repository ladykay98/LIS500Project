/ˌin(t)ərˌsekSHəˈnalədē/

Intersectionality is an analytical framework that describes how multiple social identities—such as race, gender, class, and sexuality—overlap and interact to create unique experiences of discrimination or privilege. [1, 2]

(Source: Oxford Languages)

The term was coined in 1989 by Kimberlé Crenshaw, a civil rights advocate and legal scholar, to explain how Black women face compounded forms of prejudice that cannot be understood through racism or sexism in isolation. [1, 2]

/imˈplisət ˈbīəs/

Implicit bias, also known as implicit prejudice or implicit attitude, is a negative attitude, of which one is not consciously aware, against a specific social group. Implicit bias is thought to be shaped by experience and based on learned associations between particular qualities and social categories, including race and/or gender. Individuals’ perceptions and behaviors can be influenced by the implicit biases they hold, even if they are unaware they hold such biases.

(Source: American Psychological Association).

During the course, we were encouraged to take the IBT (Implicit Bias Test) and I took the Native American one. My initial thoughts (K. Hill) about the test were that I wasn’t convinced this was an accurate assessment of one’s implicit bias although it could suggest particular leanings. Simply because of the processing delay one naturally would experience when switching the keyboard association mid-test.

The test then assumes that a delayed reaction suggests a bias. When actually a delayed reaction could simply mean that you are rewiring the keyboard associations in your brain in real time. Even though this is a study with a long history and facilitated by Harvard researchers, I still maintain my thoughts on this. In attempting to further look into the history and validity of the study I came across two noteworthy points of contention. One is that several reddit threads exist arguing the very same thing. And in one article published on the American Psychological Association’s website (article), it suggests that although the IBT raises an important point of study, the test itself is ‘not ready for prime time’ and may actually cause harm if it’s utilized by the mainstream to form conclusions that have consequences or if it’s used to shape public policy.

With that being said, I do think the conversation surrounding implicit bias and intersectionality is a worthwhile one. Human nature likes to place people in neat little boxes. It helps us quickly identify, categorize and form conclusions. And sometimes split-section judgements are necessary. However, when it comes to ensuring that all persons are treated fairly and justly, awareness of and consideration for how our society assigns certain characteristics to certain groups and the potential harm that inflicts is worth significant exploration.

Here are some additional resources you can explore to further your knowledge and understanding of intersectionality:

Kimberlé Crenshaw - "Mapping the Margins: Intersectionality, Identity Politics, and Violence against Women of Color"
While Kimberle Crenshaw coined the term "intersectionality" her work was built on the foundation of previous scholars, writers and activits.

  • The Combahee River Collective Statement (1977) — Authored by a collective of Black socialist feminists, this foundational text articulated the concept of "interlocking oppressions" over a decade before the term intersectionality was officially coined.
  • "Matrix of Domination" by Patricia Hill Collins (1990) — An essential sociology excerpt from Black Feminist Thought explaining how structural power operates across intersecting lines of race, class, and gender.
  • "There Is No Hierarchy of Oppressions" by Audre Lorde (1983) — A brief, powerful essay illustrating why identity and civil rights issues cannot be separated or ranked against one another.

Mike's Reflections on Intersectionality

I agree with Kamaria's statement that "human nature likes to put people in neat little boxes." Even though we cherish our individual experiences, we are placed in a box simply based on how we look or how we identify.

Many of us who grew up in the West spent much of our education looking through a Euro-centric lens. In the US, we're taught about the founding of our country through the lens of the Pilgrims and white people fighting in the American Revolution. And through the antebellum, Civil War, Reconstruction, both World Wars, and the 20th century, much of what is taught to us is focused on the impacts of white people on our history.

Of course, we are taught about Harriet Tubman's Underground Railroad, the woman's suffrage movement, the Civil Rights era with Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. and Malcolm X. But the impact that POCs made on American History is briefly skimmed over.

I mean, it took us til this decade to recognize Juneteenth! In school, I never learned about Black Wall Street in Tulsa, Oklahoma and the 1921 Massacre that took place there. I had to learn that through a documentary I stumbled upon.

So learning through a Euro-centric lens has an effect on POCs who grew up in the West. We are taught that the standard we should be chasing is white success. That we should be like the Rockefellers or the Vanderbilts. That success should follow the path of white culture.

As one of Asian descent, I've always heard the term "model minority." And growing up Filipino-American, our parents taught us to assimilate in the country of our culture. Sadly, the price of that is that we lose out on being in touch with our own roots. Many Filipino-Americans, like myself, don't speak the language of our parents, and maybe lose our connection with the culture that makes us unique.

In a way, I see intersectionality as identifying our unique characteristics and assuming that those identities fit neatly in a box as created by society. However, we are measured by the Western metric of success and how well we fit into the Euro-centric lens.
