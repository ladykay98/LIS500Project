See-Food: Machine Learning Project

If our model has trouble telling the difference between a Chicago-style hot dog, a banana, and a burger with fixings, how can we expect it to fairly tell human beings apart?

Project Overview

We trained an image classification model in Google's Teachable Machine using food photos we took ourselves. We wanted to explore how accurate an AI model can be and where it falls short.

Dataset Summary

  • 8 classes: Chicago-style hot dog, burger, salad, apple, banana, onion, avocado, and "Not on the List"
  • 200+ original photos contributed by both group members
  • Images grouped by class in our GitHub repository
  • Variation in angle, lighting, background, and perspective

How to Use It

  1. Click the Launch See-Food button to start your webcam.
  2. Allow camera access in your browser if prompted.
  3. Hold up different foods (or yourself) in front of the camera.
  4. Watch the predictions and confidence bars update live.

Note: this model is experimental. Lighting, background, camera angle, and the images in our training dataset all affect the results.

Project Statement

Inspiration

My recent studies (Mike) of tech inspired me to start watching the show Silicon Valley. (I know, I'm a little late to the game.) This week, I watched the episode where Jian Yang pitches his new app "See-Food." A lightbulb clicked and I realized we were doing something similar with this project, so I decided to focus our machine learning project around food.

Mike shared his lightbulb moment with me (Kamaria) as we thought about the theme of our project, and I was quickly on board, but for different reasons. Being on a health and fitness journey, my mind went straight to practical applications. Tracking and logging food for calorie counting is a tedious burden for many dieters. If an app could identify the food in a photo, estimate the portion size, and log the calories, people would be more likely to do it and stay consistent. There are apps that read barcodes on packaging, but I have yet to find one that can do the same with a picture of a dinner plate.

For those not familiar with Silicon Valley, here is Jian Yang's pitch:

The Tool

We used Google's free Teachable Machine, which promises "a fast, easy way to create machine learning models for your sites, apps, and more – no expertise or coding required."

Having no prior experience training an AI model (Kamaria), I found the site particularly user friendly. Aside from the hiccups mentioned below, it's a great tool for introducing laypeople to datasets and model training. It's just challenging enough to make you feel accomplished without so much technical nuance that you feel overwhelmed. I could see it being very useful in a classroom.

Data

Mike gathered a collection of signature Chicago-style foods, and Kamaria chose fruits and vegetables. For anyone wondering, a Chicago-style hot dog is a beef hot dog in a bun topped with mustard, pickle, tomatoes, relish, onions, and sport peppers. Learn more about the Chicago-style hot dog.

Fun fact: ketchup isn't put on a Chicago-style hot dog because it masks the flavor of the beef. It's like putting steak sauce on a dry-aged prime ribeye.

Why burgers, hot dogs, and salads? (Mike) It was happenstance, honestly. On Veterans Day, many Chicago restaurants give free meals to those who served or are currently serving. It's the only time I ask for the veteran discount. Two of my favorite restaurants participate, and they happen to serve burgers with elaborate fixings and Chicago-style hot dogs. As a challenge to the model, I wanted to see if burgers and hot dogs with elaborate toppings would be mistaken for a salad. I also included a McDonald's Quarter Pounder with Cheese in the burger dataset.

Process and Observations

The model is very fickle about what data it accepts. It only took small JPEGs, and it could not turn videos into still shots as shown in the reference video, so we assume that only works through the webcam. We had to try multiple times to get it to accept our images. Although it prompts you to bulk upload, in reality you can only upload a few at a time.

It was not consistent in identification either. I (Kamaria) first photographed my items on a green background, like the reference video from The Coding Train, and the model identified my fruits and vegetables correctly. But when my images were merged with Mike's in a combined model, the accuracy tanked. That's why our GitHub repository has so many images: the first iteration was shot against a green background and the second against a white background.

Mike's observations: Reflecting on this whole experience, maybe going with food was a bit too ambitious in teaching a machine how to detect various food items. Including a large sample for each class may help in training the model in doing a better job of distinguishing a banana from a Chicago-Style hot dog or a burger. While Kamaria's model and my model my have functioned accurately when we separated our datasets, combining them provided a greater challenge to accuracy.

Bias and the Coded Gaze

We added "Not on the List" as a base case because, when we first trained the model without it, it looked at us through the webcam and decided we were burgers. This immediately resonated with me (Kamaria) because of Dr. Joy Buolamwini's experience with the coded gaze, which she describes in her book Unmasking AI. The coded gaze is her term for the flaws she observed in facial recognition technology.

In her experiments, the technology couldn't detect her darker-skinned face, but it could identify her lighter-skinned roommate, or her when she put on a white mask. At first I thought, "Wow, we haven't come much farther in improving facial recognition," since her book came out three years ago and her research years before that. But when Mike got the same results as me, I withheld judgment just a little longer.

Mike:When Kamaria told me that her model for fruits and legumes was accurate before incorporating my data, I thought to myself, "Is there something wrong with the data she supplied?" But then, I thought to look inward: through my own biases. Was the data I provided dominating hers for some unknown reason?

Was the algorithm doing a better job of detecting the Chicago-style hot dogs and burgers over her fruits because social media rewards exquisite pictures of overdone meals?

Look at the food photos we see on Instagram and what busts through the algorithm. It isn't just a simple photo of an apple or a banana behind a plain background. The algorithm rewards the Michelin-star chef who tops a hot dog with exotic fixings and eye-appealing toppings with more views and vanity metrics.

Is Kamaria's photos of apples and bananas worth any less than the Chicago-style hot dog or the salad with multiple layers of vegetables? Of course not. But as someone who has spent the past decade in social media marketing, the goal is to create engaging photos for the prize of a high score in vanity metrics. Because social media companies tune their algorithms to reward the content that generates higher engagement, people will engage with a product that is more done up than something that showcases simplicity.

To me, the bias doesn't just lay in the datasets that favor a certain race or look, but it's also in class as well. A photo of a Toyota Corolla on a street won't get as much engagement as a beautiful influencer living their best life on a Bali beach or a yacht off the coast of Dubai, but the simple photo may have a story that provides more substance than the superficial one of the influencer.

Conclusion

Our fun little experiment with hot dogs, fruits, and burgers is an eye-opening reminder of how easily bias sneaks into automated tech. When our model mistook our faces for burgers because it had no baseline to compare us to, it drove home Dr. Buolamwini's point: algorithms only know what they are taught to see, and they miscategorize whatever gets left out of the dataset.

Adding a "Not on the List" category with Mike's selfies fixed the glitch for our project, but it highlights a much bigger issue. If a simple classroom model can reduce human faces to a fast-food item because of a limited training set, the stakes get much higher when the same blind spots show up in facial recognition, hiring tools, surveillance, or law enforcement. Inclusive data and human accountability aren't optional before we trust AI to fairly see people, especially given the harm these systems can cause.

Mike:I also learned how important it is to gather quality data and how in some instances, using 50 photos may not be enough in accurately training a data set. And if having a bigger data set could possibly help when it comes to identifying food, imagine how it could be helpful in training AI models to properly identify faces.

Many of us non-tech people think AI and algorithms is this magical process that produces results in an instant. This project helped demystify machine learning and, at least for me, made it seem like it was more than just a talking point to either scare us that AI is a doomsday machine and a world savior at the same time. Acutally trying to train a machine, in a way, made it feel less scary or fall into a transhumanist cult that this tool is supposed to be the next glorified leader of humanity.

All it is...is a tool. And they can be either wielded for good or dangerously. But the more we learn, maybe the better society will be at wielding this tool.
